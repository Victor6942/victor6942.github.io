Who I Am

The short version — it's a long story

I'm a wanderer, wandering through this world with endless curiosity, wondering what lies beyond the next river, mountain, state, country, and question.

Just like nature itself, I've migrated quite a few times. Born in South Korea, I spread my wings across Idaho, Texas, and Oregon, and now I've found myself in Boston. From the Pacific Ocean to the Gulf of Mexico, the Atlantic Ocean, and the East Sea in Korea, I've wandered across different landscapes, cultures, and ways of life.

Along the way, I've worn quite a few hats: soldier, collegiate football player, rancher, mechanic, beekeeper, plumber, HVAC engineer, server, data analyst, and now a business analytics graduate student at Babson Business School. Some people might call that an unusual career path. I call it curiosity.

I've always believed that life is too interesting to spend it doing just one thing. Whether I'm fixing a broken water pump, leading soldiers, managing beehives, or analyzing millions of data points, I find myself asking the same question: Why does this happen, and what can I learn from it?

That's what eventually brought me into economics, analytics, and strategy. I love numbers, but what fascinates me more is the meaning behind them. A spreadsheet might tell you what happened, but understanding why it happened takes curiosity, experience, and sometimes a little imagination.

When I'm not chasing the meaning behind the numbers, you'll probably find me standing waist-deep in a river, waving a fly rod around, trying to convince a fish to eat something I tied the night before. Fly fishing has taught me something no textbook ever could: patience, humility, and the beauty of not knowing what's going to happen next. And when Mother Nature decides I should stay indoors, I sometimes write poetry. I suppose it's another way of wandering, except instead of exploring rivers and mountains, I'm exploring thoughts, memories, and questions through words.

I don't know exactly where life will take me, and that's part of the excitement. I just hope to keep learning, helping people, exploring unfamiliar places, and leaving the world a little better than I found it.

After all, I'm just a curious man trying to be the happiest man in the world.

Three Ingredient Co. started from a simple idea: the parts most people throw away are often the most nutritious for pets. As a fly fisherman in Boston, I built a pet treat brand around three ingredients, Raw, Time, and Care: single-ingredient, slow-dried treats with nothing added and nothing wasted.

The Sunshine Window

The idea for Sunshine Window came from experiencing Oregon's long, gloomy winters, where more than 200 days of cloudy and rainy weather can make natural sunlight hard to come by. I asked myself a simple question: if every house has windows to let sunlight in, what happens when there's no sunlight? Sunshine Window was designed to recreate natural daylight using LED technology, helping support the body's natural circadian rhythm, which regulates sleep, energy, and mood. What started as a simple observation became an entrepreneurial venture, winning the 2025 Winterim Competition and reaching the 2025 InventOR finals.

My Fishing Journey

Every river has a story, and I'm still learning to read it.

There's a reason I call it fishing, not catching.

To me, fly fishing has never been just about catching fish. It's about returning to the basics, disappearing from the noise of everyday life, and remembering that the world is much bigger than whatever problems are waiting for me back home.

Standing in a river, I don't need to worry about emails, AI, or what tomorrow might bring. Instead, I find myself watching the falling leaves drift along the current in autumn, noticing the first newborn sprouts pushing through the soil in spring, or listening to the water make its way around the rocks. Sometimes, I spend hours or days without catching a single fish, yet somehow, I return home happier than when I left.

That's the beauty of fishing.

Being a fly fisherman has also taught me to become a better observer of Mother Nature. The temperature of the water, the insects dancing above the surface, the direction of the wind, and even the smallest changes in the current all tell a story. The more time I spend outdoors, the more I realize how much I still don't know. And that curiosity is what keeps bringing me back.

I tie my own flies, too. There's something special about sitting down with a few feathers, some thread, and a hook, trying to imitate a tiny creature that Mother Nature has spent millions of years perfecting. Sometimes the fish agree with my creation. Most of the time, they remind me who's smarter.

But perhaps the greatest lesson fly fishing has taught me is that not everything in life needs to have a measurable outcome to be meaningful. We live in a world that constantly asks us to be productive, move faster, and achieve more. Yet a river never seems to be in a hurry. The trees don't compete to grow taller overnight, and the fish certainly don't care about my résumé.

Sometimes, while whistling to the fish and watching my fly drift along the current, my mind begins to wander. I find myself thinking about everything from how I could improve a project at work to how I could become a better person, help someone, or approach life's challenges from a different perspective. Funny enough, some of my best ideas come when I'm miles away from my laptop, surrounded by nothing but trees, water, and a few fish that refuse to cooperate. But other times, I don't think about anything at all. No problems to solve, no questions to answer, no plans to make. Just me, the river, and Mother Nature doing what she does best. And perhaps those are the moments I enjoy the most.

Sometimes, the most valuable thing I can do is simply stand there, breathe the cold air, and appreciate being alive.

I may spend my professional life searching for meaning behind the numbers, but out on the river, I've learned that some of life's most beautiful things cannot be measured.

Poems

Questionable poetry, as promised

Big City, the Boston
Lots of stop signs, yet nobody seems to stop. Horns singing louder than the birds' hymns, tall buildings standing shoulder to shoulder, blocking the wind's melody. Thousands of feet marching through the streets, rushing toward somewhere, without noticing the penny on the ground. Perhaps everyone is chasing a dollar. But beyond the concrete and noise, the earth still sings her song. Summer has been falling, turning green into gold, leaving her warmth behind, and slipping into a colorful sweatshirt that somehow matches my old flannel. The trees slowly undress, one leaf at a time, painting the ground with colors no artist could ever afford. On a quiet pond, I paddle alongside a pair of swans. They glide through the water, and for a moment, I am reminded that eternal love still exists. Above us, the geese are heading south, their farewell echoing across the sky, warning us that winter is coming. The wind grows colder, the days grow shorter. I would rather walk at the speed of the seasons.
I Know, But Not Fully
I know who I am at least enough to walk forward, but not enough to explain it clean. Some days I sit in class, but my mind is already outside, somewhere between trees that don't ask questions. Out there, I feel like just another young life on this earth, nothing special, nothing above just present. The trees don't judge me, they just stand hundreds of years deep in silence, watching something like me pass through. And the river carved by ice ages I can't even imagine, moving without trying, never asking where it's going. That's where I start to understand it's not about the result. It's just the process breathing, standing, casting a line, letting time move whether I want it to or not. I get smaller out there, in front of rock that came from fire, in front of water that shaped everything before me. And somehow That's where I feel the most like myself. Because life real life isn't built on what we chase, but on the things we forget to notice that have been holding everything together this whole time.
Sitting on Top of Mount Hood, Looking Down on Portland Covered in Clouds
Over my boots, the pumice holds the day's warmth, Breathing it back slowly through the soles. The wind smells like snow that has decided to wait. Below me, clouds gather in the valley, Soft as unmade beds, Hiding streets, traffic lights, late fights with your girlfriends. The city disappears politely. To the west, the sun slips behind the ridgeline, Handing its work to the moon without ceremony. Stars arrive early, One by one, like quiet agreements. An eagle cuts across the air, Black and certain, Not interested in arguments. Far down yonder, voices rise inside the clouds People choosing sides, Chasing the sun or the moon, Calling each other wrong While missing the evening entirely. The mountain does not listen. Neither do the trees. I keep a smile on my face.
