Who I Am

The short version — it's a long story

I'm a wanderer, wandering through this world with endless curiosity, wondering what lies beyond the next river, mountain, state, country, and question.

Just like nature itself, I've migrated quite a few times. Born in South Korea, I spread my wings across Idaho, Texas, and Oregon, and now I've found myself in Boston. From the Pacific Ocean to the Gulf of Mexico, the Atlantic Ocean, and the East Sea in Korea, I've wandered across different landscapes, cultures, and ways of life.

Along the way, I've worn quite a few hats: soldier, collegiate football player, rancher, mechanic, beekeeper, plumber, HVAC engineer, data analyst, and now a business analytics graduate student at Babson Business School. Some people might call that an unusual career path. I call it curiosity.

I've always believed that life is too interesting to spend it doing just one thing. Whether I'm fixing a broken water pump, leading soldiers, managing beehives, or analyzing millions of data points, I find myself asking the same question: Why does this happen, and what can I learn from it?

That's what eventually brought me into economics, analytics, and strategy. I love numbers (no, I'm not good at math), but what fascinates me more is the meaning behind them. A spreadsheet might tell you what happened, but understanding why it happened takes curiosity, experience, and sometimes a little imagination.

When I'm not chasing the meaning behind the numbers, you'll probably find me standing waist-deep in a river, waving a fly rod around, trying to convince a fish to eat something I tied the night before. Fly fishing has taught me something no textbook ever could: patience, humility, and the beauty of not knowing what's going to happen next. And when Mother Nature decides I should stay indoors, I sometimes write poetry. I suppose it's another way of wandering, except instead of exploring rivers and mountains, I'm exploring thoughts, memories, and questions through words.

I don't know exactly where life will take me, and that's part of the excitement. I just hope to keep learning, helping people, exploring unfamiliar places, and leaving the world a little better than I found it.

After all, I'm just a curious man trying to be the happiest man in the world.

My Fishing Journey

From riverbanks to grad school and back again

It started the way most good things do — by accident, standing somewhere I probably wasn't supposed to be, with a rod I barely knew how to cast. Growing up between ranch work and open country, the river was the one place that didn't ask anything of me. No orders, no scoreboard, no deadlines. Just current, patience, and the occasional tug that made the whole day worth it.

The Army taught me discipline. Football taught me how to take a hit and get back up. Ranching and beekeeping taught me that nature runs on its own schedule, not yours. Fly-fishing taught me all of that at once, quietly, standing knee-deep in cold water with a line in my hand.

These days I tie my own flies — badly at first, less badly now — and treat every new pattern like a tiny engineering problem with feathers. Whether I'm in a classroom running regressions or standing in a river running out of daylight, the goal is the same: pay attention, stay patient, and let the current decide.

  • The ranch years — hay, horses, and learning to read weather before a screen ever told me to.
  • Boots on the ground — military service, discipline, and a different kind of patience.
  • Hives & honey — beekeeping on the side, because apparently one unpredictable hobby wasn't enough.
  • Finding the river — picked up a fly rod and never really put it back down.
  • Tying my own flies — turns out feathers and thread are cheaper therapy than most things.
  • Babson — business analytics grad school, trading rivers for spreadsheets (most days).

Poems

Where I Could
Where I could be a boy again by the riverbend, no-clock, no-name just me and the slow-moving water. Grassland-wide feeling. Like everything ahead, nothing behind. Boots half-wet, slipping down a slickenslide bank, hands in the brush-tangle, mud on my palms like it meant something. Sky full of thunderheads — big, swollen, almost breaking. The kind that make you feel small but somehow more alive. Where I could cast a line and think that was enough. The reel-click-prelude, that quiet before something pulls or doesn't. Didn't matter. A culvert nearby humming low, darkling water running through it, like some hidden path only the fish understood. Me — kid-of-the-river, he-who-waits-too-long — standing there trying to magnify the moment, hold it, stretch it, like time had a flex to it. Sometimes I'd just sit. Watch the glister on the surface. Think about nothing. Or everything. Where I could stay like that forever — no questions, no answers, just the feeling that life was wide open and I hadn't messed it up yet. This is not a time to think. Just cast. Just stand there. Let the current decide.
I Know, But Not Fully
I know who I am — at least enough to walk forward, but not enough to explain it clean. Some days I sit in class, but my mind is already outside, somewhere between trees that don't ask questions. Out there, I feel like just another young life on this earth, nothing special, nothing above — just present. The trees don't judge me, they just stand, hundreds of years deep in silence, watching something like me pass through. And the river, carved by ice ages I can't even imagine, moving without trying, never asking where it's going. That's where I start to understand — it's not about the result. It's just the process: breathing, standing, casting a line, letting time move whether I want it to or not. I get smaller out there, in front of rock that came from fire, in front of water that shaped everything before me. And somehow — that's where I feel the most like myself. Because life, real life, isn't built on what we chase, but on the things we forget to notice that have been holding everything together this whole time.
